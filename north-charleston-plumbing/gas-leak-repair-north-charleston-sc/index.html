    Outdoor Gas Line Repair in North Charleston

    Grills, fire pits, pool heaters and patio heaters often sit at the end of a long buried line that nobody has looked at since it went in.     Underground Gas Line Repair in North Charleston

    Yard lines run from the meter to the house, and often on to a detached garage, a pool heater, a grill or an outdoor kitchen.     Gas Line Repair and Replacement in North Charleston, SC

    Black iron pipe with threaded fittings is still the most common interior gas pipe, and the leak is nearly always at a thread, not in the middle of a length. We take the joint apart, clean the threads and remake it with fresh gas-rated sealant. Cracked fittings get replaced. Badly rusted sections, often near a crawlspace vent, are cut back to clean pipe and replaced. Every remade joint gets a detector check and a pressure test.

    Propane Gas Leak Repair in North Charleston, SC

    Propane behaves differently from natural gas because it is heavier than air, so a leak settles into basements and crawlspaces instead of rising. We check both regulators, the one at the tank and the one at the house. Low spots get sniffed first. If the supplier owns the tank, they handle the tank side and we handle the piping from there to the appliances. Rural properties with underground tanks get a line test too.

    Commercial Gas Leak Services in North Charleston

    Restaurant kitchens and small shops run more appliances on bigger pipe, often 1-1/4 inch or larger, fed from a meter with its own regulator. When the leak is small enough, we plan the shutdown for after close. Fryers on quick-disconnect hoses fail often. Each hose and manifold joint gets tested, and we tag what we isolated so your staff knows what is off. New pipe still needs the commercial sign-off.

    Residential Gas Leak Detection in North Charleston

    A gas leak in a house rarely sits where the smell is strongest, because gas drifts along joists and collects in closets and basements. The first step is a pressure gauge on the piping, which tells us whether the system is actually losing gas. Then the sniffer comes out. A combustible gas detector follows the reading from fitting to fitting until it spikes. Bubble solution on the suspect joint gives the final answer.
