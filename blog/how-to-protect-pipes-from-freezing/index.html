    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next South Carolina Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    Where South Carolina Homes Freeze First

    Pipes do not freeze evenly through a house. The first ones to go are the lines that sit in cold air with no heat nearby, such as a sink on an outside wall or a vented crawlspace. Hose bibs are the classic failure. Their water sits just inches from outside air.

    Pipes in crawlspaces, exterior walls and unheated garages are the ones most likely to burst during a South Carolina cold snap, since many homes were built with little insulation around them.

    Ice rarely splits the pipe at the frozen spot. What actually bursts the line is pressure trapped between the ice and a shut faucet, so the split often shows up away from the frozen part. Once the ice melts, a split half-inch line can release several gallons a minute. The crack often sits inside a wall, quiet, until the thaw.

    Why a Frozen Line Does So Much Damage

    A burst supply line keeps running at full house pressure, usually somewhere between 50 and 80 psi, until somebody shuts the main valve. Overnight, that can mean hundreds of gallons in a wall cavity. Drywall and insulation soak it up first. Mold can start on wet drywall within two days if it is not dried out.

    Steps That Keep Pipes From Freezing

    Start outside, since that is where most freeze calls begin. Pull every garden hose off its spigot before the first hard frost. Leaving a hose attached to a frost-free sillcock keeps water in the stem, which is exactly what the valve cannot handle. Older spigots without the frost-free stem need an inside shutoff closed and the outside valve left open to drain.

    • Slip foam sleeves over exposed lines in crawlspaces and garages.
    • Seal gaps where pipes pass through exterior walls with spray foam or caulk.
    • Close crawlspace vents for the winter, and open them again in spring.
    • Open sink cabinet doors on outside walls during a hard freeze.
    • Keep the thermostat at 55 or higher, even when you are away.

    On the coldest nights, let a faucet on an outside wall drip. A thin, steady drip keeps water moving and gives pressure somewhere to go if the line starts to freeze. Drip both hot and cold. With a single-handle faucet, set it in the center position.

    Heat tape is worth the money on any line you already know freezes every single winter, like a crawlspace run under a bathroom. Buy a self-regulating cable with a UL listing, not the cheap constant-wattage kind. It belongs on a GFCI outlet, and it should get a quick test every fall. Never wrap tape over itself.

    If Your Pipes Freeze Anyway

    Turn on the faucet the frozen line feeds. Only a trickle, or nothing, means you have found the frozen run. Warm the pipe with a hair dryer or space heater, starting at the faucet end and working back toward the ice. Never use a torch. Open flame inside a wall has burned down more houses than frozen pipes have flooded.

    Know where your main shutoff is before you need it. Look where the water line comes into the house, usually the garage or crawlspace, or check the meter box by the street. Turn it off the moment you see water. Our emergency line at KBN Plumbing is (864) 588-3813, and we can usually have someone headed your way within an hour.

    Long-Term Fixes for Lines That Keep Freezing

    A pipe that freezes twice will freeze again. Moving the line or insulating the wall around it is what actually solves it. During a remodel we can shift a supply line from an outside wall to an inside wall, and that ends the problem permanently. PEX also handles a freeze better than copper, since it can swell a little without splitting. It is not freeze-proof, though.

    Our plumbers winterize homes all over South Carolina and can check a crawlspace or attic for exposed lines in about an hour. The visit before winter costs a fraction of drying out a flooded ceiling. Vacant homes need extra care. We also install frost-free sillcocks and add shutoff valves to lines that never had one.
