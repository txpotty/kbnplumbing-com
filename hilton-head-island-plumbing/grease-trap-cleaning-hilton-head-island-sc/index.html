    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Hilton Head Island

    Figure $150 to $400 for an indoor trap and $300 to $1,200 for an outdoor interceptor. A recurring schedule lowers each visit. Traps that go a long time between pump-outs take longer to break up and pump, so the same unit can cost noticeably more after a long gap than it would on a steady interval.

    It depends on trap size and menu. Commercial kitchens usually land between every two weeks and every three months. Homes with a small unit may go a year, but a fryer-heavy kitchen on a 50-gallon trap might need pumping every other week.

    Slow sinks come first. Then gurgling floor drains and a sour smell near the dish area. By the time gray water is coming up through a floor sink, the trap is usually well past 25 percent full and grease is already heading down the line.

    Two things, a full pump-out and a trap that drains properly when we leave. We do not skim and call it done. Should a problem tied to our work show up later, the crew returns to fix it, and the depth readings on the sheet show how we left the trap.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. You do need to keep the records. Installing a new trap or interceptor, or changing the size of one, usually needs a plumbing permit and sometimes a pretreatment program review before the work starts.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. Disposing of it is where people get in trouble. Many programs want a licensed hauler's record for every cleaning, and a kitchen doing its own has nothing to show the inspector.

    Fryer volume drives the schedule more than seat count. Plenty of busy restaurants pump monthly. A coffee shop with a light menu may go two or three months, provided the depth readings stay below 25 percent.
